VCode: a Multimodal Coding Benchmark with SVG as Symbolic Visual Representation

This research introduces VCode, a novel benchmark that reframes multimodal understanding as visual-centric code generation, requiring models to produce SVG code from images to preserve symbolic meaning for downstream reasoning. A key strength lies in its innovative evaluation protocol, CodeVQA, which assesses symbolic fidelity by having a policy model answer questions over rendered SVGs, covering challenging domains such as commonsense, professional knowledge, and visual-centric perception. To address inherent difficulties, the paper proposes VCoder, an agentic framework augmenting Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with "Thinking with Revision" for iterative refinement and "Acting with Visual Tools" to integrate structured visual cues, achieving substantial performance gains over strong baselines. However, a significant weakness is that even frontier VLMs, including top performers, still notably struggle to generate faithful SVGs, revealing a persistent gap in visual-centric coding, particularly in professional knowledge and 3D reasoning tasks. Furthermore, specialist SVG models underperform, and the highest scores are often achieved when relying on linguistic descriptions as an intermediate step, suggesting an inherent limitation in direct visual-to-symbolic conversion despite VCoder's improvements.

When Visualizing is the First Step to Reasoning: MIRA, a Benchmark for Visual Chain-of-Thought

The MIRA benchmark introduces a novel approach to evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) by focusing on complex reasoning tasks that intrinsically require the generation and utilization of intermediate visual images, mirroring human "drawing to think" problem-solving. A key strength is its comprehensive dataset of 546 challenging multimodal problems across 20 task types, meticulously annotated with ground-truth intermediate visual steps, enabling a robust three-level evaluation protocol (direct, text-CoT, and visual-CoT). The research demonstrates that state-of-the-art MLLMs, including powerful proprietary models, perform poorly when relying solely on textual prompts, often seeing performance degradation with text-based Chain-of-Thought. However, a significant strength highlighted is that when provided with human-annotated visual cues, model performance consistently and substantially improves, yielding an average relative gain of 33.7%, underscoring the critical role of visual information in these tasks. A primary weakness, however, lies in the current state of MLLMs: while MIRA evaluates the *utilization* of visual Chain-of-Thought, models cannot yet autonomously *generate* these intermediate visual artifacts, meaning the benchmark assesses a simulated "imagination" rather than true "thinking while drawing." Furthermore, even with expanded search spaces and specialized text prompts, the limited improvements suggest a fundamental capacity gap in MLLMs for this type of complex visual reasoning, rather than merely accidental errors.

Don't Blind Your VLA: Aligning Visual Representations for OOD Generalization

This research article, "Don’t Blind Your VLA: Aligning Visual Representations for OOD Generalization," systematically investigates how standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on robotic tasks leads to a significant degradation of their inherited Vision-Language (VL) understanding and visual representations, ultimately impairing out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. The authors rigorously demonstrate this through attention map analyses showing "attention sink," t-SNE visualizations revealing "representation collapse," and a newly introduced diagnostic benchmark called VL-Think, which uncovers domain-specific forgetting in VLAs. To mitigate this issue, they propose a lightweight Visual Representation Alignment method, inspired by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, that anchors the VLA's internal visual features to a frozen, generalist vision teacher using a simple regularization loss. A key strength of this work lies in its comprehensive empirical analysis of degradation and the proposal of a practical, computationally efficient solution that consistently yields improved OOD generalization across various visual, semantic, and execution challenges in robotic environments. However, a notable weakness is that while the alignment method effectively recovers visual-semantic consistency and partially restores VL capabilities, particularly for concrete concepts like color and shape, it shows limited success in fully mitigating domain-specific forgetting for more abstract concepts underrepresented in the robotic fine-tuning datasets, hinting at inherent limitations from data diversity and LoRA architectural constraints.

When Modalities Conflict: How Unimodal Reasoning Uncertainty Governs Preference Dynamics in MLLMs

This research article proposes a novel framework for understanding how Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) resolve conflicts when modalities provide contradictory information, a process termed "modality following." The authors move beyond coarse dataset-level statistics by decomposing this behavior into "relative reasoning uncertainty" (the case-specific confidence gap between unimodal predictions, measured by entropy) and "inherent modality preference" (a stable bias when uncertainties are balanced). A key strength is the development of a controllable dataset that systematically varies visual and textual reasoning difficulty, allowing them to discover a "universal law": the probability of an MLLM following a modality monotonically decreases as its relative uncertainty increases. The "balance point" on this curve offers a principled way to quantify inherent preference, disentangling it from unimodal capabilities and dataset artifacts. Furthermore, layer-wise probing provides mechanistic insight, revealing internal "oscillations" between conflicting choices in ambiguous regions, explaining external indecision. While the framework offers robust quantitative and mechanistic insights into MLLM decision dynamics, a potential weakness is its reliance on a synthetic "toy dataset" for controlled experiments, which, despite its benefits for isolating variables, may limit the direct generalizability of specific findings (like balance points or oscillation frequencies) to the full complexity of real-world multimodal data. Additionally, while claiming a "universal law," the validation is primarily across two MLLM families and the provided datasets, suggesting broader validation across more diverse architectures and real-world benchmarks would further strengthen this claim.

Shorter but not Worse: Frugal Reasoning via Easy Samples as Length Regularizers in Math RLVR

This research tackles the issue of excessive verbosity in large language model (LLM) reasoning by challenging the standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) practice of discarding "easy" training problems. The authors demonstrate that retaining and modestly up-weighting moderately easy problems acts as an implicit length regularizer, leading to "emergent brevity" where models solve complex problems concisely without explicit length penalties. Applied to a 4B model, this approach maintains or slightly improves baseline accuracy on mathematical benchmarks like AIME25 while nearly halving solution lengths, demonstrating superior efficiency, especially on harder problems, a benefit highlighted by their new Efficiency-Adjusted Accuracy (EAA) metric. However, its scope is limited to math reasoning with verifiable rewards and a single 4B model, restricting generalization to open-ended or larger-scale scenarios. Additionally, the emergent brevity is empirically observed without a robust theoretical explanation, a recognized area for future work along with exploring adaptive curricula and combined regularization.

Brain-IT: Image Reconstruction from fMRI via Brain-Interaction Transformer

Brain-IT introduces a novel brain-inspired framework for reconstructing images from fMRI, utilizing a Brain Interaction Transformer (BIT) that processes functional brain-voxel clusters shared across subjects to predict both high-level semantic and low-level structural image features. Its primary strength lies in achieving state-of-the-art performance, significantly improving the faithfulness and visual quality of reconstructed images compared to prior methods. The system effectively integrates information from these localized features, guiding a diffusion model that is initialized by a Deep Image Prior based on low-level structural cues, resulting in robust reconstructions. Furthermore, Brain-IT demonstrates exceptional efficiency in transfer learning, capable of producing meaningful results with as little as 15 minutes of new subject data and achieving performance comparable to methods trained on 40-hour datasets with just 1 hour of fMRI data. However, a notable weakness is that despite these advancements, the reconstructed images are not perfectly accurate, occasionally lacking fine-grained details or precise semantic content, a limitation the authors suggest may reflect the inherent constraints of the fMRI signal itself or the expressiveness of current feature spaces.

TWIST2: Scalable, Portable, and Holistic Humanoid Data Collection System

TWIST2 presents a novel, portable, and scalable system for holistic humanoid robot data collection and autonomous control, addressing the lack of effective data frameworks in humanoid robotics. It achieves full whole-body teleoperation by leveraging low-cost PICO4U VR technology (approx. $1000) and a custom $250 robot neck for essential egocentric stereo vision, moving away from expensive motion capture setups or decoupled control methods. This innovative design enables highly efficient data collection, allowing for hundreds of successful demonstrations of long-horizon dexterous and mobile manipulation tasks in short periods, and supports a hierarchical visuomotor policy framework for autonomous, vision-based whole-body control in real-world tasks like pick & place and dynamic kicking. A significant strength is its single-operator, fast setup (1 minute), and open-sourced nature, promoting reproducibility and wider adoption. However, the system's limitations include the general motion tracker's difficulty with highly dynamic human movements such as sprinting, and the PICO VR's pose estimation being less accurate than high-cost motion capture systems, particularly for joints like elbows and knees that lack direct trackers, which can affect the fidelity of collected motion data.

LTD-Bench: Evaluating Large Language Models by Letting Them Draw

LTD-Bench introduces a novel benchmark that addresses a critical blind spot in LLM evaluation by revealing fundamental spatial reasoning limitations often concealed by traditional numerical metrics. Its primary strength is transforming LLM assessment from abstract scores to intuitively observable visual outputs, as models are tasked with generating drawings via dot matrices or executable code, making spatial reasoning deficiencies immediately apparent even to non-experts. The comprehensive methodology includes complementary generation (spatial imagination) and recognition (spatial perception) tasks across three difficulty levels, robustly evaluating bidirectional language-spatial mapping. Experiments expose alarming capability gaps in state-of-the-art LLMs regarding language-spatial mapping, undermining their potential as genuine world models, while also demonstrating LTD-Bench's utility for diagnostic analysis and investigating model similarity via visual outputs. Acknowledged weaknesses include a relatively small dataset, a narrow focus solely on spatial perception and imagination limiting generalizability, and a preliminary model similarity analysis based merely on stylistic comparisons.

The Collaboration Gap

This preprint introduces a novel, scalable maze-solving benchmark to empirically evaluate AI-AI collaboration, uniquely isolating this capability by distributing maze information and employing an LLM-based auto-grader to handle unconstrained communication. Through extensive evaluation of 32 leading models in solo, homogeneous, and heterogeneous pairings, the research robustly identifies a "collaboration gap," revealing that models proficient in solo tasks often degrade significantly when required to collaborate, with distilled models being particularly affected. The study further demonstrates that interaction order is critical, proposing and validating "relay inference," where a stronger agent initiates the dialogue, substantially boosting collaborative performance for weaker partners. While the benchmark effectively highlights the necessity of explicit collaboration training for AI agents and offers practical strategies for improving inter-agent dynamics, the simplified maze environment, inherent non-determinism of LLMs, and occasional parser limitations in fully accommodating truly unconstrained agent behaviors (e.g., unintended maze schemas or agents "splitting up") represent acknowledged limitations. The high computational cost associated with such large-scale multi-turn evaluations also presents a barrier to replication.

Can Visual Input Be Compressed? A Visual Token Compression Benchmark for Large Multimodal Models

This preprint introduces UniPruneBench, a comprehensive and unified benchmark designed to systematically evaluate visual token pruning methods in large multimodal models (LMMs). Its primary strength lies in addressing the fragmented and inconsistent evaluation landscape by standardizing protocols across an extensive range of ten datasets covering six ability dimensions, ten representative compression algorithms, and three major LMM families (LLaVA-v1.5, Intern-VL3, and Qwen2.5-VL). Notably, UniPruneBench incorporates crucial system-level metrics such as runtime and prefilling latency alongside task accuracy, providing a holistic assessment of efficiency. While offering a valuable, reproducible framework, the benchmark's findings also expose weaknesses in the current state of visual token compression: it reveals that random pruning is a surprisingly strong baseline, challenging the efficacy of many designed strategies; it demonstrates that no single method consistently outperforms others, indicating a lack of universal solutions; and it highlights significant task-level sensitivity, with OCR being highly vulnerable to pruning, suggesting limitations in current methods' robustness. Furthermore, the research indicates that naive combinations of pruning strategies do not yield additive gains, pointing to a need for more sophisticated, deliberate design in multi-stage compression.

BRAINS: A Retrieval-Augmented System for Alzheimer's Detection and Monitoring

BRAINS (Biomedical Retrieval-Augmented Intelligence for Neurodegeneration Screening) is a novel system developed to enhance early Alzheimer's disease detection and monitoring, particularly in regions with limited diagnostic resources. It features a dual-module architecture combining a Large Language Model (LLM)-based cognitive diagnostic module, fine-tuned on diverse neurocognitive and neuroimaging data, with a case retrieval module that identifies and integrates similar patient histories via a Case Fusion Layer. A significant strength of BRAINS is its ability to overcome the token length limitations inherent in standard Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, allowing for the effective fusion of up to five auxiliary cases, which enhances contextual understanding and diagnostic precision for multi-morbidity inference. The system demonstrated superior performance, achieving 77.3% accuracy in classifying disease severity and identifying early cognitive decline on real-world datasets, substantially outperforming a baseline LLaMA2-13B model, which showed poor reliability, especially in single-label prediction tasks. While BRAINS effectively utilizes derived neuroimaging metrics, a minor limitation is its design choice not to directly process raw neuroimages, and the practical mechanisms behind its claimed "explainable outputs" could benefit from more explicit detail for clinical users. Nevertheless, BRAINS offers a promising, scalable, and robust framework for interpretable Alzheimer's disease screening.

CodeClash: Benchmarking Goal-Oriented Software Engineering

CodeClash introduces a novel benchmark that evaluates language models (LMs) in goal-oriented software engineering by pitting them against each other in multi-round tournaments across diverse competitive coding arenas. The benchmark successfully uncovers LMs' capacity for creativity and varied development styles in an open-ended adversarial setting, alongside their improved proficiency in command-line interactions. However, the research highlights significant weaknesses, revealing that LMs possess fundamental limitations in strategic reasoning, struggling to adapt after losses and consistently misinterpreting competitive feedback, often hallucinating reasons for failure or making untested code changes. A major drawback is also their inability to manage codebases long-term, leading to progressively messy and redundant repositories, which ultimately results in a substantial performance gap where even top LMs fail to secure a single win against expert human programmers.

iFlyBot-VLA Technical Report

iFlyBot-VLA is a novel Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model designed to improve robotic manipulation by combining a dual-level action representation with a multi-stage mixed training strategy. Its primary strengths lie in its ability to predict both latent actions, which capture high-level intentions from extensive human and robotic manipulation videos, and structured discrete action tokens for explicit low-level control, aligning vision, language, and action representations. The model effectively preserves and enhances the VLM backbone's general perceptual and reasoning capabilities by training on a diverse dataset that integrates general and spatial question-answering data with large-scale robot manipulation trajectories, preventing the degradation often seen in action-only VLM training. Utilizing a flow-matching diffusion transformer for continuous action generation, iFlyBot-VLA demonstrates superior performance on the LIBERO benchmark and achieves competitive success in real-world tasks, including generalizable pick-and-place, long-horizon parcel sorting, and dexterous dual-arm cloth folding, with plans to open-source parts of its dataset. Despite these advancements, the model, consistent with imitation learning approaches, exhibits limitations when confronted with novel instructions, entirely unseen objects or concepts, and out-of-distribution inputs, hindering its ability to recover effectively in such scenarios, which the authors suggest could be addressed by incorporating reinforcement learning in future work.

VidEmo: Affective-Tree Reasoning for Emotion-Centric Video Foundation Models

VidEmo introduces an innovative affective cues-guided reasoning framework for emotion-centric video foundation models, unifying fundamental attribute perception, expression analysis, and high-level emotional understanding through a two-stage training process involving curriculum emotion learning and affective-tree reinforcement learning. A major strength is its superior performance, consistently outperforming existing open and closed-source VideoLLMs, including Gemini 2.0, across 15 diverse facial perception tasks by providing fine-grained, interpretable emotional captions with explainable rationales. This is further bolstered by the introduction of Emo-CFG, a meticulously curated, large-scale (2.1M samples) emotion-centric dataset featuring hierarchical annotations and rationale-grounded data, validated for high quality via committee voting and user studies. However, the model's limitations include its susceptibility to generating counterfactual content, potentially leading to false narratives, and its current reliance solely on video modality, overlooking other crucial multimodal cues like audio that are essential for holistic emotion understanding.

ChartM^3: A Multi-Stage Code-Driven Pipeline for Constructing Multi-Dimensional and Multi-Step Visual Reasoning Data in Chart Comprehension

This research introduces ChartM3, a novel dataset for complex chart comprehension, constructed through an automated multi-stage, code-driven pipeline designed to enhance multimodal large language models' (MLLMs) visual reasoning capabilities. The pipeline's strengths lie in its systematic generation of diverse and high-quality data, leveraging Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for professional chart templates and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) strategies to produce realistic data distributions and detailed reasoning paths for 62 chart types and 142K Q&A pairs. Experiments demonstrate that training MLLMs on ChartM3 significantly improves their reasoning and cross-domain generalization, enabling smaller models to achieve performance comparable to larger ones, further validated by successful supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) applications for chart understanding. However, the study has limitations: the chart rendering code is primarily Python-based, limiting support for other visualization languages; it focuses exclusively on statistical charts, omitting other visual formats like flowcharts; and the reinforcement learning experiments were not conducted at a larger scale, suggesting potential for further exploration.

TabDSR: Decompose, Sanitize, and Reason for Complex Numerical Reasoning in Tabular Data

The TABDSR framework addresses the challenges of complex numerical reasoning in tabular data for large language models (LLMs) by employing a three-agent pipeline: a query decomposer for breaking down complex questions, a table sanitizer for cleaning noisy input, and a Program-of-Thoughts (PoT)-based reasoner for generating executable code to derive answers. A significant strength of TABDSR is its consistent state-of-the-art performance, demonstrating substantial accuracy improvements across multiple benchmarks like TAT-QA, TableBench, and the novel, leakage-mitigated CalTab151 dataset, proving effective even with powerful LLMs like GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 and highlighting its practical transferability and lower adoption barriers. However, the system's weaknesses include its continued shortfall compared to human performance and inherent reliance on the underlying LLM's reasoning abilities; furthermore, the CalTab151 dataset's small size and limited question types constrain comprehensive evaluation, while the query decomposer's question-only focus can occasionally degrade performance, and the table sanitizer's efficacy is limited by the base model's reflection capabilities, sometimes leading to persistent table-cleaning errors.

D2D: Detector-to-Differentiable Critic for Improved Numeracy in Text-to-Image Generation

This preprint introduces D2D (Detector-to-Differentiable), a novel framework addressing the persistent challenge of generating accurate object counts in text-to-image diffusion models. Its core strength lies in ingeniously transforming traditionally non-differentiable, high-performing object detectors into differentiable critics using custom activation functions and optimizing the initial noise via a Latent Modifier Network during inference. This approach significantly boosts object counting accuracy across various complexity levels (up to 13.7% improvement on benchmarks) and object types, effectively correcting both over and under-generations with minimal degradation in image quality and computational overhead. The method demonstrates broad generalization across different T2I model backbones like U-Net and Transformer, and detector types like OWLv2 and YOLOv9. However, a notable weakness is its continued struggle with high-density and multi-object scenarios, where performance drops significantly for larger counts. Additionally, D2D is not designed for fine-grained layout control or object placement, and some challenging object categories (e.g., transparent items) show more limited improvement.

LiveSecBench: A Dynamic and Culturally-Relevant AI Safety Benchmark for LLMs in Chinese Context

LiveSecBench is a highly relevant AI safety benchmark specifically designed for Chinese-language LLMs, effectively addressing the limitations of static, English-centric evaluations by offering a dynamic, continuously updated assessment rooted in Chinese legal and social frameworks. Discriminately Treating Motion Components Evolves Joint Depth and Ego-Motion Learning

This research introduces DiMoDE, an unsupervised joint learning framework for depth and ego-motion that addresses the limitations of previous methods which often indiscriminately mixed different motion types. DiMoDE proposes a discriminative treatment of motion components (rotation, tangential, and radial translation) by leveraging their distinct geometric regularities. It achieves this by aligning optical axes and imaging planes of cameras, transforming optical flows, and imposing individual geometric constraints based on deviations from expected rigid flows. This approach reformulates joint learning into coaxial and coplanar forms, allowing mutual derivation of depth and translation components through closed-form geometric relationships, which introduces robust supervisory signals for depth estimation, particularly in challenging conditions. Strengths of DiMoDE include achieving state-of-the-art performance in both depth estimation and visual odometry, demonstrating enhanced robustness and convergence stability across diverse and challenging real-world environments (e.g., overexposure, motion blur, camera shake), and being compatible with various network architectures while maintaining a lightweight design for real-time inference. However, the framework's weaknesses include continued struggles under extremely low illumination due to degraded visual cues, and a limited capacity to generalize effectively to truly *unseen* highly complex and diverse real-world environments beyond those it was exposed to during training.
